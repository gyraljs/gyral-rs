# Structure and coherence

Use when the reader can understand individual sentences but cannot follow the passage. This reference draws principally on chapter 5. Its examples and editing procedures are original applications.

## Establish topic and point

The topic is what the passage concerns; the point is what it says or accomplishes about that topic. “Data retention” names a topic. “We need different retention periods for audit logs and temporary exports” supplies a point.

Make both recoverable reasonably early. Suspense can be deliberate in a story or essay; a decision memo rarely benefits from hiding its recommendation. If drafting discovers a new point, revise the introduction to prepare for it.

Select a progression the reader can anticipate. Possibilities include an item moving through a process, a question and its explanation, comparisons along fixed dimensions, or a decision supported by evidence. The order in which the author found the material need not become the reader's route through it.

## Track three connections

1. **Topic continuity:** what each sentence is about and why attention shifts.
2. **Entity continuity:** how names, pronouns, descriptions, and event nouns identify recurring things.
3. **Propositional relations:** how a claim explains, qualifies, contrasts with, or otherwise develops another.

An outline handles nested organization; it does not capture every connection. An answer may refer back to a question many paragraphs earlier. Make such links recoverable without repeatedly restating the entire argument.

## Identify the relation before choosing the connective

| Relation | What it does | Useful signals when needed |
|---|---|---|
| Similarity | Attributes a shared property. | also, similarly, parallel syntax |
| Contrast | Compares a difference on a shared dimension. | but, in contrast, alternatively |
| Elaboration | Supplies detail about the same subject or event. | a colon, specifically, that is |
| Exemplification | Moves from a general claim to an instance. | for example, including |
| Generalization | Moves from cases to a broader claim. | more generally, in general |
| Exception, generalization first | Identifies a case outside an established pattern. | however, except |
| Exception, exception first | Establishes the broader pattern despite a case. | nevertheless, usually |
| Temporal sequence | Orders events. | before, after, then |
| Result | Presents cause followed by effect. | so, as a result |
| Explanation | Presents effect followed by cause. | because, owing to |
| Violated expectation | Presents an obstacle followed by an outcome it might prevent. | but, yet, nonetheless |
| Failed prevention | Presents an outcome followed by the unsuccessful obstacle. | despite, even though |
| Attribution | Identifies whose position or claim is being stated. | according to, argues that |

These relations are a useful map, not an exhaustive taxonomy. Connectives can have multiple uses. *While* can indicate time or contrast; choose a more explicit alternative if that ambiguity matters.

A connective states a relationship; it does not prove it. If “therefore” cannot be justified, the problem may be a missing premise or an invalid argument. Repairing transitions must not turn correlation into causation or a reported opinion into the author's position.

Experts can supply connections that beginners cannot. When the relation is already obvious, an added connective may be redundant. When it is uncertain, favor a clear signal, but signal one relation only once.

## Preserve referents and controlled comparisons

Repeat an exact term when variation could imply a different object. A pronoun works when its referent is identifiable. A generic substitute works when readers can easily connect it to the earlier specific object. Do not replace a precise object with a more specific label later unless the new information is established.

Use definite descriptions carefully. *The error* presumes an identifiable error; *an error* introduces one. If several errors have appeared, name the relevant one.

Hold wording and measurement constant in comparisons wherever possible. If one option's success rate is compared with another's failure rate, the reader must perform an unnecessary conversion. Convert only when the denominators and categories support it; otherwise explain the difference.

Original comparison:

> “Option A retains logs for seven days; option B retains logs for thirty days.”

The repeated structure highlights the intended variable. Stylistic variation would add little here.

## Make negation interpretable

A denial needs a plausible proposition to deny. If the reader has no reason to think the cache is permanent, beginning “The cache is not permanent storage” may create an unnecessary detour. “The cache stores temporary copies” supplies the relationship directly. If the reader actually holds the misconception, the denial is useful.

Inspect both **scope** and **focus**:

- Scope: “Not every request failed” differs from “Every request failed.”
- Focus: “Mara did not approve the change” might correct who approved it, what happened, or which change was approved. Supply the intended contrast when context does not.

Negative meaning can hide in words such as *avoid*, *deny*, *rarely*, or *prevent*. Several negatives may reverse the intended sense. Paraphrase the actual outcome to check it, but retain a direct prohibition when that is the intended message.

Original ambiguous sentence:

> “We did not publish because the review was finished.”

Possible meanings require different revisions:

> “We published because the deadline arrived, not because the review was finished.”

> “Because the review was finished, we decided not to publish the preliminary version.”

Do not choose between these without evidence of the intended event.

## Check proportion and thematic consistency

Readers infer importance from space, emphasis, and position. A minor digression that occupies half a report can displace its main question. Shorten it, subordinate it, or move it to a reference section if the task permits.

Keep a theme's terms visibly related. Introduce a new conceptual cluster before relying on it. A sequence of erudite names or abstractions is not coherent merely because the author knows their relationship.

Paragraph breaks are reader aids, not proof of a fixed logical formula. Place them where readers need to absorb a unit, recognize a turn, or recover their place. Do not enforce a universal sentence count.

## A practical structure review

Write a short description of each paragraph's function. Read the descriptions in sequence. Locate the topic, point, evidence, qualification, and action. For each transition, identify the relation without relying on a decorative “moreover.”

If a paragraph has no role, determine whether it supplies a missing premise, belongs elsewhere, or is unnecessary. If two paragraphs have the same role, merge or distinguish them only when that improves the result. Preserve deliberate pacing in essays and personal writing.

# Sentences and usage

Use for difficult syntax, word order, ambiguity, or a contested edit. Principles draw on chapters 4 and 6; the examples are original. For logical relationships and negation, see [structure and coherence](structure-and-coherence.md).

## The web, tree, and string

A writer has a web of ideas, selects a tree of phrases and clauses, and presents a string of words. A reader receives the string and must reconstruct the other two.

Inspect two burdens separately: finding the intended grammatical grouping and holding unfinished constructions in memory. A sentence can be grammatical but difficult on either measure. Shortening is one possible repair, not the diagnosis itself.

## Find the head and the unfinished construction

A phrase's head helps determine how the phrase fits into the sentence. A nearby noun can distract from that relationship:

> “The list of available rooms is on the desk.”

Here, *list* determines singular agreement. Treat this as a diagnostic for this construction, not as a complete theory of agreement: coordination, notional agreement, and idiom can complicate the picture.

Flat coordination and rightward elaboration often allow readers to finish one unit before handling another. Heavy material at the beginning can delay identifying the subject or claim. Center embedding can force readers to suspend several constructions at once.

Original example:

> “The report that the analyst whom the director hired prepared was incomplete.”

> “The analyst prepared an incomplete report. The director had hired the analyst.”

The split reveals the propositions but does not establish that both belong in the final text. Determine why the hiring matters. If it does not, omit that fact; if it does, connect it. Do not mechanically split every sentence.

## Prevent wrong grammatical turns

Look for phrases that can attach to more than one neighbor, modifiers with unclear scope, and words that initially suggest the wrong grammatical category. A sentence that becomes clear at the end may still impose avoidable rereading.

Small words can be valuable boundaries:

> “The manager confirmed the payment had arrived.”

> “The manager confirmed that the payment had arrived.”

The second signals early that the confirmation concerns a proposition. Retain *that* when it improves parsing; omit it when the shorter form is equally clear and suits the rhythm.

Noun piles often conceal relationships. “Customer account recovery approval policy” could refer to several different policies. Identify which relation is intended before expanding it with prepositions or clauses.

## Check parallelism by substitution

Each coordinated part should fit the construction it shares:

> “The tool supports importing records and to export labels.”

> “The tool supports importing records and exporting labels.”

For *both … and*, *either … or*, and *not only … but also*, inspect the phrase after each marker. Moving a marker may be enough to restore parallel structure. Do not impose identical wording when the meanings genuinely differ.

## Balance given/new and light/heavy

Given/new concerns the reader's knowledge and attention. Light/heavy concerns phrase length and complexity. These are distinct dimensions.

Place familiar context before a new claim when that helps the reader attach the information. Place heavy material where it does not interrupt an unresolved construction. Neither preference automatically wins: an urgent action may need to lead; a contrast may require a different emphasis.

Available tools include:

| Tool | Original illustration | Possible effect |
|---|---|---|
| Passive voice | “The mural was restored by the conservators.” | Keeps the affected object in focus and moves the actor later. |
| Fronted modifier | “After the outage, the queue drained.” | Establishes a frame before the main event. |
| Extraposed clause | “It is surprising that the older design performed better.” | Delays a long clausal subject. |
| Existential construction | “There is a spare battery in the cabinet.” | Introduces an entity. |
| Cleft | “It was Nadia who found the error.” | Focuses on the finder while presupposing someone found it. |
| Pseudo-cleft | “What we need is a smaller example.” | Gives a frame before its completion. |
| Alternative verb perspective | “Mara sold the bicycle to Jon” / “Jon bought the bicycle from Mara.” | Changes the participant used as the starting point. |

Added words may pay for clearer sequencing. Clefts also introduce presuppositions; do not smuggle in an unestablished fact with an elegant construction.

## Choose passive voice by context

Use passive voice when the affected entity is the topic, the actor is irrelevant or unknown, or postponing a long actor phrase improves processing. Prefer naming the actor when responsibility matters. Active voice can also hide agency through vague subjects; grammatical voice is not a complete test of accountability.

“The door was red” describes a property and is not passive. “The door was painted” is a typical passive construction. Searching for *was* is not a reliable way to diagnose passives.

## Judge usage on separate grounds

Ask whether an objection concerns meaning, grammatical structure, established usage in the relevant variety, house style, or rhetorical effect. Description of language and practical prescription can coexist. A convention can be useful without being an inherent law of logic.

Selected distinctions from the book:

- **Restrictive versus supplementary clauses:** “The invoices that are overdue appear in red” selects a subset. “The invoices, which are overdue, appear in red” adds a comment about the identified invoices. Punctuation changes the claim. Restrictive *which* is not inherently wrong, though a publication may prefer *that*.
- **Less versus fewer:** plural discrete items often favor *fewer*. Measurements and singular constructions do not follow a blanket prohibition on *less*. “Less than ten minutes” is not repaired by changing it to “fewer than ten minutes.”
- **Split infinitives and final prepositions:** evaluate clarity, emphasis, and register rather than enforcing absolute bans. A permissible construction can still be awkward in a particular sentence.
- **Actual word distinctions:** checking folklore does not make *credible/credulous* or *mitigate/militate* interchangeable.
- **Usage changes:** do not elevate the book's 2014 preferences or usage-panel results into universal current rules. Check an appropriate current authority when a live dispute matters, following the host's research requirements.

Do not invent a historical explanation for a usage rule. Not every disputed prohibition has the same origin.

## Punctuation reveals structure imperfectly

Punctuation helps readers recover grouping and some features of spoken rhythm, but it is not a complete transcription of pauses. A long subject does not normally need a comma before its predicate simply because the writer would pause there.

For two independent clauses, choose the connection deliberately:

> “The road is closed. The delivery is delayed.”

> “The road is closed; the delivery is delayed.”

> “The road is closed, so the delivery is delayed.”

The last asserts causation; use it only if warranted. A colon can introduce an explanation or elaboration when the structure permits it. Respect local conventions for quotation punctuation, spelling, and other stylistic choices.

## Read for sound without editing by sound alone

Read a difficult passage aloud or simulate its cadence. Notice where intended emphasis or pauses carry meaning unavailable on the page. Repair that gap through wording or appropriate punctuation. Retain rhythm, deliberate repetition, and meaningful variation when they improve the experience.

Avoid exchanging every vivid word for a more common one or every long sentence for several short ones. The goal is recoverable meaning and a suitable voice, not uniform sentence geometry.

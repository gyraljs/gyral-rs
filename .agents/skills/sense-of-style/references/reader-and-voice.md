# Reader, stance, and voice

Use when establishing an explanation, addressing knowledge gaps, reducing abstraction, or preserving an author's character. This reference operationalizes the prologue and chapters 1–3, with the treatment of nominalization also drawing on chapter 5. See [sources](sources.md) for attribution and evidential limits.

## Treat style as more than correctness

Clarity helps meaning travel; care in prose can earn trust; rhythm, imagery, and surprise can make reading pleasurable. Preserve all three where appropriate. A technically correct sentence can still be lifeless or confusing. An elegant sentence can still be false.

Study successful prose by naming a choice and its effect. Look for a meaningful opening, fresh detail, parallel construction, selective uncommon words, sound suited to mood, and an ending that lands on something worth remembering. Borrow a technique only if it serves this piece; do not copy a voice by applying a list of surface mannerisms.

## Choose the communication stance

| Stance | Assumption | Appropriate use |
|---|---|---|
| Classic | A capable equal can see what the writer is presenting from a useful vantage point. | Showing and explaining an interesting truth. |
| Practical | Reader and writer have defined roles and a concrete task. | Procedures, requests, decisions, reference material. |
| Plain | The relevant truth is straightforward to state. | Direct statements where development adds little. |

Classic style does not mean short, informal, or free of abstract ideas. It can make an abstraction intelligible by presenting relationships the reader can inspect. It does not require every document to use a narrative opening.

The classic stance that the writer knows what to show describes the finished presentation. Drafting can still discover the point. Revise the opening when the point becomes clearer later.

## Supply knowledge without underestimating intelligence

An expert in one field may need elementary background in another. Define the missing concept rather than reassuring the reader that it is “easy,” “obvious,” or “nothing to worry about.” Conversely, avoid explaining what the specified audience already knows without a contextual reason.

Diagnose three related problems:

- **Curse of knowledge:** the author treats private knowledge as available to the reader.
- **Chunking:** several familiar steps are compressed into one label; the reader may not share that package.
- **Functional fixity:** the author names an object by its professional role and forgets that the reader needs to identify the actual thing.

Ask what the reader must already know for a sentence to work. Missing knowledge may be a definition, a scene, a boundary around a term, a prerequisite, or a causal link. Supply the smallest sufficient explanation, not a beginner's lecture by default.

Original example:

> “Present the access artifact” could mean a badge, a QR code, or a document.

Once the intended object is known:

> “Show the attendant the QR code in your confirmation email.”

The gain comes from resolving the referent. If the artifact's actual form is unknown, ask; do not invent it.

## Balance explicitness with cooperation

Readers infer. An author need not narrate every obvious connection. But the author's feeling that a step is obvious is weak evidence that the reader can supply it.

Add definitions or connectives where the reader's knowledge makes them necessary. Remove them where they repeat an unmistakable relation or suggest a distinction that does not exist. Test an uncertain explanation through a reader's paraphrase or task performance when available. Do not claim to have tested it if you only reviewed it yourself.

Feedback need not come from a perfect representative reader to reveal a problem. Still, distinguish what one reader found confusing from what the entire audience will find confusing. A delayed reread can help an author recover distance from familiar wording.

## Use abstraction deliberately

A technical noun can summarize a useful concept. A nominalization can refer economically to an event already introduced. Problems arise when the writer starts with compressed labels before the reader has seen the event.

Original pair:

> “The coordinator postponed the workshop. The postponement gave instructors another week to prepare.”

Keeping *postponement* is sensible: it refers back to an understood event. Replacing every noun with a verb would make the passage less economical.

When encountering *framework*, *issue*, *level*, *process*, or *approach*, ask whether it names an actual distinction. If it does, define that distinction. If it only wraps an action in ceremony, name the action. Do not apply a forbidden-word list.

## Keep uncertainty informative

A qualifier earns its place when it identifies uncertainty, scope, population, conditions, or degree that changes the claim. An intensifier can be just as vague as a hedge: *very effective* needs a criterion.

Original draft:

> “The new layout may be somewhat faster.”

If timing evidence exists, report what was measured and under which conditions. If it does not, preserve uncertainty: “We expect the new layout to reduce completion time, but have not measured it.” Use that wording only if it accurately describes the author's position.

Do not remove *may*, *in this sample*, or *under these conditions* to make an argument sound bold. Do not add invented numbers to replace vague wording.

## Keep attention on the right subject

Metadiscourse describes the presentation itself. Use it when it helps navigation or establishes an essential boundary. Avoid requiring readers to memorize unfamiliar section names before knowing the subject.

Professional activity is relevant when the piece concerns the development of a field, a disagreement, or the methods behind a finding. Otherwise, an opening about growing interest among researchers may delay the phenomenon the reader came to understand.

With clichés or mixed metaphors, picture the scene. Check whether the image clarifies the relationship and whether its parts fit. Scare quotes do not rescue an expression the writer is unwilling to use directly. Preserve quoted wording when it is actually a quotation or when discussing the term itself.

## Preserve the author's voice

Before a substantial rewrite, notice the author's preferred vocabulary, degree of formality, cadence, humor, certainty, point of view, and imagery. Distinguish a working stylistic choice from a comprehension problem.

Keep a colloquial phrase if it conveys the intended meaning to the audience. Keep a purposeful fragment if the genre supports it. Change a long sentence when its structure loses the reader, not merely because it is long. Keep repeated words when they carry rhythm or preserve a referent.

If voice is underspecified for new writing, use the user's supplied examples or stated preferences. Ask for a sample only when matching a personal voice is central and existing context is insufficient. Otherwise choose a suitable register and avoid claiming an exact voice match.
